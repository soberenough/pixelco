What to Put on a Freelance Invoice: A Line-by-Line Checklist With an Example

An invoice has one job: get you paid, in full, on time, without a single follow-up question. Most late payments don't start with a client who refuses to pay. They start with an invoice that is missing something small: no purchase order number, a vague description the approver can't match to a project, no clear due date, or payment details buried in an email from three months ago. The invoice then sits in someone's inbox while they wait to ask you about it.

This guide walks through every line a freelance invoice should have, why each one matters, a complete worked example, and the small habits that make invoices get paid faster. It is general information, not tax or legal advice. Invoice rules differ by country and by whether you are registered for sales tax or VAT, so check your local requirements or ask an accountant before you rely on any template for tax purposes.

The short answer: the 12 things every freelance invoice needs

  1. The word "Invoice" at the top
  2. A unique invoice number
  3. The invoice date (the date you issue it)
  4. A due date, written as an actual date
  5. Your name or business name, address and contact email
  6. Your client's legal or business name, address and the name of your contact
  7. The client's reference: purchase order (PO) number, project code or contract name
  8. Line items that describe the work in words the client will recognise
  9. Quantity and rate for each line (hours, days, deliverables or a fixed fee)
  10. Subtotal, any tax, any discount or deposit already paid, and the total due
  11. How to pay you: accepted methods and the details needed for each
  12. Your payment terms, including any late fee you agreed in your contract

Everything else is optional. The sections below explain each item and the mistakes that cause delays.

1. Header and invoice number

Write "Invoice" clearly at the top. If you are sending a quote, estimate or pro forma, label it as that instead, because finance teams process them differently and an unlabelled document can end up in the wrong queue.

Every invoice needs a unique number that you never reuse. It lets both of you refer to the invoice without confusion, and it is how you will track what has been paid. Three simple systems work well:

  • Sequential: 0001, 0002, 0003. Simple, but it shows new clients how many invoices you have sent.
  • Year plus sequence: 2026-014. Easy to sort and resets each year.
  • Client code plus sequence: ACME-007. Handy if you have a few long-term clients.

Pick one and keep it. If you make a mistake on a sent invoice, don't edit it and resend it under the same number. Issue a corrected invoice with a new number, or a credit note against the old one, and say which invoice it replaces.

2. Dates: issue date and due date

The invoice date is the day you send it. The due date is the day the money should arrive. Write the due date as a calendar date, for example "Due 15 December 2026", not only as "Net 30". Many people don't know what "Net 30" means, and even those who do have to work out the date themselves. A plain date removes the guesswork and makes a follow-up email much easier to write.

Your due date should match what your contract or proposal says. If you never agreed terms, choose something reasonable and state it clearly. Fourteen or thirty days is common for freelance work; shorter terms are fine for small jobs and new clients.

If the work covers a period of time, add the service period too, such as "Work completed 1-30 November 2026". It helps the client match the invoice to their budget month.

3. Your details and the client's details

Include your name or business name, a postal address if you use one for business, and the email address where questions should go. If you are registered for VAT, GST or a sales tax, your registration number usually has to appear as well.

For the client, use their legal or registered business name, not just the brand name or your contact's name. Larger companies often pay from an entity with a different name from the one on their website, and their accounts team may reject an invoice addressed to the wrong one. Ask during onboarding: "Who should the invoice be addressed to, and where should I send it?" Sometimes the answer is an accounts payable inbox rather than the person you work with.

4. The client's reference number

This is the line freelancers most often miss, and it causes more delays than anything else. Many companies issue a purchase order (PO) number before work starts. Their system can't approve an invoice without it. If you don't include it, the invoice goes back to your contact, who has to look it up, and you lose a week or more.

If the client doesn't use POs, include whatever helps them match the invoice: a project name, a contract date or their internal cost code. Ask for it before you start work, not when you invoice.

5. Line items that explain themselves

The person approving your invoice may not be the person who hired you. Write each line so that someone in finance can understand what was delivered without opening an email thread.

VagueClear
Design workHomepage and pricing page designs, two revision rounds (per proposal dated 3 Oct)
ConsultingStrategy workshops, 4 sessions x 2 hours, 6-27 Nov
Extra changesChange request #2: added FAQ section (approved by email 18 Nov)

For hourly work, show hours and rate on each line. For fixed-price work, a single line for the agreed fee is fine, ideally with the milestone named ("Milestone 2 of 3: first draft delivered"). If you agreed extra work along the way, give it its own line and reference the approval. That one habit prevents most invoice disputes.

6. Totals, tax, deposits and discounts

Show the arithmetic in order so it can be checked at a glance:

  • Subtotal of all line items
  • Any discount, shown as a separate negative line
  • Tax, with the rate shown, if you are required to charge it
  • Any deposit or earlier payment already received, as a negative line
  • Total due, in bold, with the currency

Always name the currency, especially with international clients. "$2,400" could mean US, Canadian or Australian dollars. Write "USD 2,400.00" or "2,400.00 EUR". If you charge tax, the rules on what must appear (rate, amount, your registration number, sometimes the client's) depend on where you and your client are, so this is the line most worth checking with a local accountant.

7. How to pay you

Put the payment details on the invoice itself, every time. Clients lose old emails, and a finance team won't go looking for your bank details. List each method you accept and the information needed for it: account name and number for bank transfer, or a payment link if you use one.

A few practical points:

  • Offer the method that is easiest for the client to use, even if it costs you a small fee. A slower, cheaper payment is not cheaper if it arrives a month late.
  • If your bank details ever change, tell clients by phone or a separate verified channel as well as on the invoice. Invoice fraud often works by sending fake "updated bank details", and careful clients will check.
  • Ask clients to put the invoice number in the payment reference so you can match payments.

8. Payment terms and late fees

Restate your terms briefly at the bottom: when payment is due and what happens if it is late. If your contract includes a late fee, mention it on the invoice in the same words. Don't invent a late fee on the invoice that you never agreed. In many places a late fee needs to be part of the agreement to be enforceable, and some countries have specific statutory rules for late payment between businesses.

A short, polite line works well: "Payment due by 15 December 2026. As agreed in our contract, invoices more than 14 days overdue incur a late fee of 2% per month." If you want to see what an agreed fee would add up to, our late fee calculator works it out.

A complete example

Here is how all of that fits together for a freelance copywriter billing a fixed-price project with one approved change and a deposit already paid.

Invoice2026-031
Issued16 November 2026
Due30 November 2026
FromJordan Lee Copy, 12 Example Street, Springfield. hello@example.com
ToNorthfield Outdoor Supplies Ltd, Accounts Payable. Attn: Sam Rivera
Client referencePO 44817
DescriptionQtyRateAmount
Website copy: home, about and 3 product category pages, 2 revision rounds (proposal dated 2 Oct)11,800.001,800.00
Change request #1: returns policy page (approved by email 4 Nov)1300.00300.00
Subtotal2,100.00
Less deposit received 6 Oct (invoice 2026-024)-900.00
Total due (USD)1,200.00

Payment: bank transfer to Jordan Lee Copy (account details below), quoting invoice 2026-031 as the reference. Terms: due within 14 days of issue, as set out in the proposal dated 2 October 2026.

The approver can see what was bought, what changed and who approved it, what has already been paid, how much is left, when it is due and how to pay it. There is nothing to ask about, so nothing to wait for.

Habits that get invoices paid faster

  • Invoice the day the work is accepted. Every day you wait is a day added to when you are paid.
  • Send a PDF, not an editable file. Name it clearly, such as "Invoice-2026-031-JordanLeeCopy.pdf".
  • Write a two-line covering email. State the invoice number, amount and due date in the email itself, so it can be forwarded without opening the attachment.
  • Confirm receipt for larger invoices. A quick "Could you confirm this reached the right person?" catches invoices sent to the wrong inbox.
  • Keep a simple tracker. Record the invoice number, client, amount, sent date, due date and paid date. When something is overdue, you will know the same day.
  • Follow up on a schedule, not a feeling. A friendly reminder a few days before the due date, then a firmer one after it, gets most invoices paid. Our guide on following up on an unpaid invoice has a timeline and wording for each step.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Missing PO number or addressing the invoice to the wrong company entity.
  • "Net 30" with no actual due date.
  • No currency shown on an international invoice.
  • Payment details missing or only in an old email.
  • Line items so vague that finance has to ask your contact what they are.
  • Reusing an invoice number or quietly editing an invoice after sending it.
  • Adding late fees or terms on the invoice that were never agreed.
