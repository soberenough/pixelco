How to Write a Cold Email That Gets Freelance Clients (With Examples)

Cold email still works for freelancers, but not the way most people try it. A long message about you, sent to fifty strangers with their first name swapped in, gets ignored. A short message about them, sent to ten carefully chosen people with a real reason to write, gets replies and sometimes gets work.

This guide walks through the whole process: who to write to, what to say, how long it should be, how to follow up, and what to do when someone replies. There are three full example emails you can adapt, and a checklist at the end to run before you hit send.

The short answer

  • Write to a specific person at a specific business you could clearly help, not to a generic inbox.
  • Open with something true and specific about them that you noticed. That is your reason for writing.
  • Connect it to one concrete thing you could do, and back it with one piece of proof (a similar project, a sample, a link to relevant work).
  • Keep it under about 120 words. Make the ask small: a reply, a yes or no, or a short call.
  • Follow up once or twice, a few working days apart, then stop.

Step 1: Pick who to write to (this matters more than the wording)

Most cold emails fail before the first word is written, because they go to the wrong people. A good prospect has three things:

  1. A visible need you can fix. An outdated website, a blog that stopped two years ago, a product page with no photos, a job ad for work you do, a new location or product launch that will need content. If you can't name the need, you don't have a prospect yet.
  2. Budget to pay for it. A business that already pays for advertising, hires staff, or has recently raised money or expanded is more likely to pay a freelancer than a hobby project.
  3. A person you can reach. The owner of a small business, or the marketing, product or operations lead at a larger one. Look on the company's own site, their team page, or professional profiles. Avoid info@ addresses when you can find a named person.

Start with a list of 20 to 30 businesses in a niche you already know. Experience in a niche is the easiest proof you have: "I've written product descriptions for three outdoor gear shops" lands much better than "I'm a versatile copywriter."

Step 2: Find your reason for writing

The single biggest difference between a cold email that gets read and one that gets deleted is the first line. It needs to show, in one sentence, that you looked at their business specifically. Good reasons for writing include:

  • Something they just did: a launch, a new hire, a new location, a rebrand, an award.
  • Something you noticed that could be better, described kindly and specifically.
  • A job ad or a post where they asked for help with something you do.
  • A real connection: a mutual contact, an event you both attended, a past client in the same industry.

Spend five to ten minutes per prospect on this. If you can't find a reason after ten minutes, move on to the next business. That's a sign the fit isn't strong.

Step 3: Write the email

A reliable structure has four parts, each one or two sentences long.

PartWhat it doesExample
ObservationShows you looked at them specifically"I saw you've opened a second shop in Leeds this month. Congratulations."
IdeaLinks what you noticed to a result they'd care about"Your site still only lists the first address, so people searching for the new one may not find opening hours or directions."
ProofGives one reason to believe you can do it"I build and update sites for independent retailers. Here's a recent one with two locations: [link]."
Small askMakes replying easy"Would it help if I sent over a short plan for adding the new shop? No charge for the plan."

A few rules that keep this tight:

  • Make it about them. Count the sentences that start with "I". If it's more than two, rewrite.
  • One idea per email. Don't list every service you offer. The goal is a reply, not a sale.
  • Be specific about proof. A link to one relevant piece of work beats a link to your whole portfolio.
  • Ask for something small. "Can we book a 45-minute discovery call?" is a big ask from a stranger. "Would a short plan be useful?" or "Is this something you're thinking about this year?" is easy to answer.
  • Plain text. No images, no attachments, no heavy signature. It should look like a normal email from a person.

Subject lines

Keep subject lines short, specific and honest. Something that reads like a normal work email does best: "New Leeds shop on your site", "Question about your product pages", "Idea for the spring catalogue". Avoid clickbait, fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" prefixes, and anything that sounds like an advert. A misleading subject line can also break email marketing rules in some countries, and it starts the relationship on a lie.

Three example emails you can adapt

Example 1: Web designer to a local business

Subject: New Leeds shop on your site

Hi Priya,

I saw you've opened a second shop on Kirkgate this month. Congratulations. Your website still only shows the original address and hours, so anyone searching for the new branch may land on the wrong details.

I build and maintain sites for independent shops, including one with three locations that I set up last spring: [link].

Would a short plan for adding the new shop be useful? Happy to send one over, no charge.

Thanks,
Sam

Example 2: Copywriter answering a visible need

Subject: Your product pages

Hi Marcus,

I was looking at your new range of trail packs. The photos are excellent, but most of the product pages have a single line of text, so details like capacity, weight and fit are hard to find.

I write product descriptions for outdoor gear brands. Here's a before and after from a recent project: [link].

If it would help, I could rewrite one page as a free sample so you can see whether the style fits. Interested?

Best,
Ana

Example 3: Developer replying to a job post

Subject: Your post about the booking system

Hi Jo,

I read your post saying the clinic's online booking keeps double-booking rooms. I've fixed the same problem for two small practices by adding a room check before a slot is confirmed.

I'm a freelance developer and can usually scope this kind of fix within a day. A short write-up of a similar project is here: [link].

Is this still open? If so, I can send a few questions to get the details right.

Cheers,
Lee

Notice what they have in common: one observation, one idea, one piece of proof, one easy question. None of them is longer than about 90 words.

Step 4: Follow up (once or twice, then stop)

Many replies come from a follow-up rather than the first email, simply because people are busy. A simple rhythm works well:

  • Follow-up 1, about 3 to 4 working days later. Reply in the same thread with one or two lines: "Just bringing this back to the top of your inbox. Happy to send that plan if it's useful."
  • Follow-up 2, about a week after that. Add something new, such as a quick tip or a relevant example, or close the loop: "I'll assume the timing isn't right and won't keep emailing. If it comes up later, I'm here."

Stop after that. More messages rarely help and can damage your reputation, especially in a small niche where people talk to each other.

Step 5: Handle replies

  • "Yes, send it over." Do exactly what you offered, quickly, and end with one clear next step, such as a short call or a quote.
  • "Not right now." Thank them, ask if you can check back in a few months, and note the date. A polite "not now" is often a future client.
  • "How much?" Give a range or a starting price and the main thing it depends on. Offer to confirm a fixed price once you know the details.
  • "Please don't contact me." Apologise briefly, remove them from your list, and don't email again.

Stay on the right side of the rules

Many countries have rules for unsolicited business email, and they differ. Common requirements include identifying yourself honestly, not using misleading subject lines, offering a simple way to opt out, and respecting opt-outs promptly. Some places treat emails to sole traders differently from emails to companies. This is general information, not legal advice, so check the rules where you and your prospects are based. Sending a small number of genuinely individual emails, rather than bulk messages, is both more effective and much less risky.

How many to send, and how to track them

Quality beats volume. Ten well-researched emails a week is a sustainable pace alongside client work, and it gives you enough data to see what's working after a month. Keep a simple tracker with these columns:

ColumnWhy
Business and contact nameSo you never email the same person twice by mistake
Reason for writingShows which kinds of openers get replies
Date sent, follow-up datesKeeps your follow-ups on schedule
ResultNo reply, not now, interested, call booked, won, or opted out
Next action and dateTurns "not now" into a reminder instead of a lost lead

After 30 or 40 emails, look at which niches and which reasons for writing got replies, and do more of those.

Checklist before you hit send

  • Is it addressed to a named person who can make the decision?
  • Does the first line mention something specific and true about their business?
  • Is there only one idea and one ask?
  • Is there one relevant piece of proof?
  • Is it under about 120 words, in plain text, with an honest subject line?
  • Have you put the follow-up dates in your tracker?

Cold email is a numbers game only in the sense that you need to send enough of them. Each individual email should still feel like it was written for one person, because it was.
