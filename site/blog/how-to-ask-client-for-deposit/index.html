How to Ask a Client for a Deposit (and What to Say When They Push Back)

Asking for money before you've done any work feels awkward the first few times. It shouldn't. A deposit is one of the most ordinary terms in professional services: builders take one, wedding photographers take one, agencies take one. It protects your calendar, filters out clients who aren't ready, and tells everyone the project is real.

The trouble is rarely the idea of a deposit. It's the wording, the timing, and not knowing what to say when a client hesitates. This guide covers all three: how much to ask for, when and where to bring it up, exact wording you can adapt, and calm replies to the five most common objections. It is general information, not legal or financial advice. If you work under a written contract, ask a local professional to review your payment terms.

The short answer

  • Put the deposit in your proposal and your contract, not in a separate awkward email afterwards.
  • State it as a standard term ("my projects start with a 50% deposit"), not as a request ("would it be OK if...").
  • Tie it to something the client wants: the start date, a reserved slot in your calendar, or the first deliverable.
  • Send the deposit invoice the same day the client says yes, and don't start work until it's paid.
  • Have one or two flexible options ready (a smaller deposit, or milestones) so a hesitant client has a way to say yes.

Why a deposit is worth asking for

A deposit does more than reduce the risk of not being paid. It changes the shape of the whole project:

  • It reserves your time. When you block out two weeks for a client, you turn other work away. The deposit pays for that reservation.
  • It confirms the client is ready. A client who can't get a deposit approved probably can't get the final invoice approved quickly either. Better to learn that on day one.
  • It covers early costs. Stock assets, software, subcontractors, travel: if you spend money to start, the deposit means you're not lending it to your client.
  • It keeps the project moving. Clients who have already paid something tend to send feedback and approvals faster, because they want to see what they've paid for.
  • It shrinks the worst case. If a project is cancelled halfway, you've at least been paid for part of the work already done.

How much to ask for

There's no single correct number, but most freelancers settle on one of these patterns:

ProjectCommon structureWhy it fits
Small project (a few days of work)50% upfront, 50% on delivery, or 100% upfrontShort projects leave little time to chase payment later. Paying in full upfront is normal for small fixed packages.
Medium project (2 to 8 weeks)50/50, or 40/30/30 at milestonesKeeps your exposure to roughly half the project at any time.
Large project (2 months or more)25 to 30% upfront, then monthly or milestone invoicesA smaller deposit is easier for the client to approve, and regular invoices stop a large unpaid balance building up.
Ongoing work or retainerFirst month paid in advanceYou're always working on time that's already been paid for.
New client with no track recordLean toward the higher endYou have no payment history with them yet.

A useful check: ask yourself how much unpaid work you could afford to lose if this client disappeared tomorrow. Your deposit plus your milestone schedule should keep your exposure below that number at all times.

When to bring it up

The best time to mention a deposit is before the client has emotionally committed to a price, so it becomes part of the deal rather than a surprise added afterwards. In practice that means three touchpoints:

  1. On the discovery call. When you talk about process, mention it in passing: "Once you're happy with the proposal, I'll send a deposit invoice for 50%, and as soon as that's in, I'll book your start date."
  2. In the proposal. Give payment its own short section, right next to the price. Show the deposit as an amount and a due date, not just a percentage.
  3. In the contract. Spell out the deposit amount, when it's due, that work starts once it's received, and what happens to it if the project is cancelled.

By the time the deposit invoice arrives, the client has seen the term three times. Nothing about it is unexpected.

What to say: wording you can adapt

In a proposal

"Payment: The project fee is $4,000. A 50% deposit ($2,000) is due on signing and reserves your start date of March 3. The remaining $2,000 is due on final delivery. Work begins once the deposit is received."

On a call or in a reply email

"That all sounds great. Here's how starting works: I take a 50% deposit to book the project into my calendar, and the balance is due when I deliver. Once the deposit is in, your start date is locked in."

In the email with the deposit invoice

"Hi Sam, thanks for signing, I'm looking forward to this one. Attached is the deposit invoice for $2,000. As soon as it's paid, I'll confirm your March 3 start and send over the onboarding questionnaire. Payment details are on the invoice; let me know if your accounts team needs anything else from me."

If the deposit is late

"Hi Sam, a quick check on the deposit invoice from last Tuesday. I'm holding March 3 for you, but I can only keep it reserved until Friday. If anything's holding up payment on your side, let me know and I'll help sort it out."

Notice what these have in common: they're short, they state the term as a fact, they connect payment to something the client wants (the start date), and they never apologise.

What to say when the client pushes back

Most pushback isn't a refusal. It's a question, a process problem, or a test. Here are the five you'll hear most and how to answer them calmly.

1. "We don't usually pay upfront."

This is often about internal process rather than trust. Ask before you negotiate.

"Understood. Is that a fixed policy, or more about how your accounts team processes things? A deposit is standard on all my projects because it reserves the time, but I'm happy to work with your process. For example, I could invoice on signing with your usual payment terms, and we'd schedule the start date for when it clears."

2. "Can you start now and we'll pay at the end?"

"I'd love to get going. The deposit is what lets me commit the time and turn other work away, so I start every project the same way. If cash flow is the concern, I can lower the deposit to 30% and split the rest across two milestones."

3. "How do we know you'll deliver?"

A fair question from someone who hasn't worked with you. Answer it with proof, not by dropping the deposit.

"Completely fair. You'll get a signed agreement that sets out exactly what's delivered and when, and I'll share progress at each milestone so you're never waiting in the dark. Happy to put you in touch with a past client too." Only offer a reference if you have one who has agreed to it.

4. "Our payment terms are Net 60."

Large organisations often can't change their payment cycle, but they can usually change when an invoice is raised.

"No problem. In that case I'll send the deposit invoice the day the contract is signed so it's moving through your system early, and we'll set the start date to suit. I'll also invoice each milestone on completion rather than waiting for the end."

5. "Another freelancer didn't ask for a deposit."

"That's fine, everyone works differently. I take a deposit on every project because it means I can block out the time properly and give your project my full attention when it's scheduled. It's part of how I keep my deadlines."

If a client still won't accept any form of upfront payment, milestone billing or a lower deposit, take that as useful information. A client who resists every protective term at the start is unlikely to become easier to work with once the work is done.

Flexible options that still protect you

  • Lower deposit, more milestones. 25% upfront with three milestone payments keeps your exposure small while easing the client's first payment.
  • Paid first phase. Sell a small, fixed-price discovery or strategy phase paid in full upfront. It works as a trial for both of you and builds trust for the bigger project.
  • Start date on payment. If their process is slow, don't fight it. Just agree that the schedule starts when the deposit clears.
  • Weekly or fortnightly invoicing. For hourly or open-ended work, short billing cycles do much of what a deposit does.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Starting before the deposit lands. "They said it's on its way" is not paid. Once you've started, the deposit loses most of its purpose.
  • Asking apologetically. "Sorry, would it be possible to maybe..." invites negotiation. State it as how you work.
  • Leaving it out of the contract. Your contract should say whether the deposit is refundable, and under what conditions, if the project is cancelled. Unclear terms here are a common source of disputes.
  • Using percentages only. "50% deposit" leaves room for confusion. "$2,000 due March 1" doesn't.
  • Waiving it for friends or "great opportunities". The projects you feel too awkward to ask a deposit on are often the ones that go unpaid.

A simple checklist

  1. Decide your standard deposit for small, medium and large projects.
  2. Add one sentence about it to your discovery call notes.
  3. Add a payment section with amounts and dates to your proposal template.
  4. Make sure your contract covers the deposit amount, due date, start condition and cancellation.
  5. Prepare a deposit invoice template and a short covering email.
  6. Save the five pushback replies above somewhere you can reach quickly.

Once a deposit is simply part of how you work, you'll find most clients pay it without a second thought.
