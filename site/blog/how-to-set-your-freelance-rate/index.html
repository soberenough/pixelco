How to Set Your Freelance Rate: A Step-by-Step Method

Most new freelancers set their rate by looking at what others charge, or by dividing their old salary by 2,080 hours. Both approaches leave money on the table. The first ignores your costs. The second assumes you'll bill every working hour, which no freelancer does. This guide walks through a method that starts from what you actually need, then adjusts for the market.

Step 1: Start with the take-home pay you need

Write down the amount you want to keep each year after tax. Be honest: include savings, retirement contributions, and the things your employer used to pay for, like paid leave. If you're replacing a salary, a common starting point is your old salary plus 20–30% to cover the benefits you no longer get.

Step 2: Add your business costs

List everything the business pays for in a year:

  • Software subscriptions and tools
  • Equipment, replaced every few years (divide the cost by its lifespan)
  • Health, liability and equipment insurance
  • Accounting and legal help
  • Training, courses and books
  • Workspace, phone and internet (the business share)
  • Marketing: website, portfolio hosting, networking events

For many solo freelancers this lands somewhere between a few thousand and fifteen thousand a year. Don't guess low. Unexpected costs always appear.

Step 3: Gross it up for tax

Your take-home goal is after tax, so you need to earn more than that before tax. A simple approximation is to divide your take-home goal by (1 minus your tax rate). With a 28% combined rate, a 60,000 take-home goal needs about 83,300 of profit before tax. Self-employed people often pay more than employees because they cover both sides of social-security-style contributions, so check the real figure for where you live.

Step 4: Work out your real billable hours

This is the step that changes everything. A full-time year is about 2,080 hours, but you won't bill most of them. Take out:

  • Time off. Holidays, public holidays and sick days. Six weeks is a realistic total for many people.
  • Unbilled work. Finding clients, writing proposals, invoicing, admin, learning. Most freelancers bill only 50–70% of their working hours.

At 40 hours a week, 46 working weeks and 60% billable, you have about 1,100 billable hours a year. That's barely half the 2,080 most people assume.

Step 5: Add a buffer and calculate

Add 10–15% for quiet months, late payers and clients who disappear. Then:

Before-tax profit needed83,300
Plus business costs8,000
Plus 10% buffer100,400 revenue
Divided by billable hours1,104
Minimum hourly rateabout 91

Step 6: Check it against the market

Your calculated number is a floor, the least you can charge and still meet your goals. Now compare it with what clients in your field pay:

  • Ask freelancers a year or two ahead of you what they charge. Most will share a range.
  • Look at published rate surveys for your industry and region.
  • Note what clients say when you quote. If everyone says yes instantly, you're probably too cheap.

If the market pays well above your floor, charge market rates. If it pays below your floor, you have three choices: niche down into work that pays more, raise your billable share, or reconsider the goal.

Step 7: Choose how to package it

An hourly rate is a calculation, not necessarily how you have to quote. Many freelancers earn more by quoting:

  • Day rates for on-site or intensive work. They avoid haggling over minutes.
  • Project prices based on the value to the client. Estimate your hours, multiply by your rate, then add margin for risk and revisions.
  • Retainers for ongoing work. A set monthly fee for a set amount of availability gives you predictable income.

Step 8: Review it twice a year

Raise your rate for new clients first, then for existing ones with 30–60 days' notice. Good triggers for a raise: you're fully booked, your costs went up, or you've added a skill clients value. A steady annual increase is normal and expected.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing your old salary by 2,080 and calling it your rate
  • Forgetting tax and quoting a number you'll never take home
  • Assuming every working hour is billable
  • Competing on price with the cheapest freelancers online
  • Never raising rates for long-term clients

Getting paid on time matters as much as the rate itself. Clear payment terms and a simple tracking habit make a real difference to your cash flow.
