How to Follow Up on an Unpaid Invoice: A Timeline and What to Say

An invoice is overdue and you're not sure how hard to push. Chase too gently and you wait months; chase too hard and you damage a relationship that might be worth years of work. The answer is a calm, predictable sequence: the same steps, at the same intervals, for every client. This guide gives you that sequence, with what to say at each step and what to do if nothing works.

First, check it's really late

Before you send anything, spend two minutes making sure the problem isn't on your side. A surprising number of "late" invoices were never properly received.

  • Was it sent to the right person? Many organisations route invoices to an accounts address, not to the person who hired you.
  • Does it have everything their accounts team needs? A purchase order number, their full legal name, your business details, a unique invoice number, a due date, and clear payment details.
  • What were the agreed terms? "Net 30" means 30 days from the invoice date, not from when you finished the work. If you agreed "net 60", it isn't late yet.
  • Is the client waiting on something from you? An unanswered question or a missing final file can quietly hold up approval.

If anything is missing, fix it and resend the invoice with a short note. The payment clock effectively restarts, but you've removed the reason it was stuck.

The follow-up timeline

This timeline assumes standard 14- or 30-day terms. Each step is a little firmer than the one before, but none of them is rude. Consistency is what works: clients quickly learn that you always follow up, and your invoices move to the top of the pile.

Step 1: The friendly reminder (3 days before it's due)

This isn't chasing; it's service. It catches problems (wrong address, missing PO number) while there's still time to fix them.

Subject: Invoice [number] due on [date]

Hi [Name], a quick heads-up that invoice [number] for [project] is due on [date]. I've attached it again for convenience. Let me know if your team needs anything else from me to process it. Thanks!

Step 2: The overdue notice (1 day after)

Keep it short and neutral. Assume it's an oversight, because it usually is.

Subject: Invoice [number] is now overdue

Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount] was due yesterday and I haven't seen the payment come through yet. Could you check where it is? The invoice is attached, and payment details are on page one. Thanks very much.

Step 3: Second reminder and a phone call (7 days overdue)

At this point, ask a question that needs a specific answer. "Just checking in" is easy to ignore; "When can I expect payment?" is not.

Subject: Payment date for invoice [number]?

Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount] is now a week overdue. Could you let me know the date it's scheduled for payment? If there's an issue with the invoice or the work, I'd rather hear about it now so we can sort it out.

The same day, call or message the person who hired you, and if you can, the accounts contact too. A two-minute friendly call often solves in one go what three emails didn't: the invoice was sitting in someone's inbox, waiting for an approval nobody knew about.

Step 4: The firm reminder (14 days overdue)

Now you state the consequence. If you're still doing work for this client, this is the point to pause it, politely.

Subject: Invoice [number]: 14 days overdue

Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount] is now two weeks overdue, and I haven't had a payment date yet. As set out in our agreement, I'll need to pause further work on [project] until it's settled. I'd like to keep things moving, so please let me know today when payment will be made.

Only mention late fees or interest if your contract includes them, or if the law where you work allows them without a contract term. Check the rules that apply to you before you add any charge.

Step 5: The final notice (30 days overdue)

This is the last message before you escalate. Give a specific deadline and say exactly what happens next. Then do what you said.

Subject: Final notice: invoice [number]

Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount] is now 30 days overdue. Despite my emails on [dates], I haven't received payment or a payment date. Please pay the full amount by [date, 7-10 days from now]. If it isn't received by then, I'll begin the formal recovery process. I'd much rather resolve this directly, so please reply if there's anything we need to discuss.

If the client says they can't pay right now

A client who replies is a client you can work with. If they're short on cash, a written payment plan usually beats a long dispute:

  • Agree specific amounts and specific dates, in writing (email is fine).
  • Ask for a first payment straight away, even a small one. It confirms the plan is real.
  • Keep work paused until the balance is cleared, or until the plan has run smoothly for a while.
  • Agree what happens if a payment is missed: the full remaining balance becomes due.

If the client disputes the work

Sometimes silence hides a complaint. If they say the work wasn't what was agreed, separate the issue from the payment:

  • Ask them to put the specific problem in writing.
  • Compare it with the written scope. If it's a fair point, fix it quickly and say so.
  • If part of the work is undisputed, ask them to pay that part now while you resolve the rest.

A clear written scope, agreed before you start, is the best protection against this. If you don't use one yet, start with your next project.

When reminders don't work: your options

If the final deadline passes, you have several options. Which ones are available, and how they work, depends on where you and your client are based, so treat this as general information and check the rules that apply to you.

  • A formal letter of demand. A short, formal letter stating the amount, the work it relates to, a final deadline, and the action you'll take. Some people have a solicitor or lawyer send it, which often prompts payment on its own.
  • Mediation. Some regions offer free or low-cost mediation for small business disputes.
  • Small claims. Many countries have a simplified court process for smaller amounts, designed to be used without a lawyer. Fees and limits vary.
  • A collection service. These typically keep a percentage of what they recover. Read the terms carefully.
  • Writing it off. For very small amounts, your time may be worth more. If you do, record it properly in your books, and don't work with that client again without payment upfront.

How to get paid on time in the first place

The best follow-up is the one you never need to send. A few habits prevent most late payments:

  1. Agree payment terms before you start, in writing: the amount, the due date, how to pay, and what happens if payment is late.
  2. Take a deposit. 25-50% upfront for new clients is common and filters out people who were never going to pay.
  3. Invoice the day the work is delivered. Every day you wait is a day added to the end.
  4. Ask who processes invoices and what they need on them, before you send the first one.
  5. Make paying easy. Clear payment details, a couple of options, and the total in large type.
  6. Bill larger projects in stages so you're never owed more than you can afford to lose.
  7. Track every invoice with its due date and your next follow-up date, so nothing slips.

The short version

Check the invoice was right and reached the right person. Then follow the same sequence every time: a reminder before it's due, a neutral notice the day after, a direct question and a phone call at one week, paused work at two weeks, and a final notice with a deadline at 30 days. Stay polite, keep everything in writing, and do exactly what you said you'd do.
