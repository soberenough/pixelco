How to Write a Freelance Scope of Work (With a Template and Example)

Most freelance disputes aren't about quality. They're about what was included. The client thought "a website" meant twelve pages and a blog; you quoted for five pages and no blog. The client expected unlimited tweaks; you priced two rounds of changes. Neither of you is lying. You simply never wrote it down in a way you both read the same way.

A scope of work (often shortened to SOW) fixes that. It's a short document, usually one or two pages, that says exactly what you will deliver, what you won't, how long it will take, and what happens when something changes. This guide walks through the eight sections a freelance scope of work needs, gives you a template to copy, shows a complete example, and covers the mistakes that cause the most trouble.

This is general information, not legal advice. A scope of work usually sits alongside a contract that covers payment, ownership and liability. If you work on large projects or across borders, have a lawyer look at your contract once.

Scope of work vs. proposal vs. contract

These three get mixed up, so it helps to separate them:

  • Proposal: sells the project. It explains the client's problem, your approach, and why you. It can be persuasive.
  • Scope of work: defines the project. It lists deliverables, limits, timeline and responsibilities. It should be boring and precise.
  • Contract: governs the relationship. It covers payment terms, ownership of work, cancellation, confidentiality and liability.

On small projects, all three can live in one document. That's fine, as long as the scope section is clearly separated and specific. On bigger projects, keep the scope of work as its own attachment so you can update it without rewriting the contract.

The 8 sections every freelance scope of work needs

1. Project summary (two or three sentences)

State what the project is and what it's for, in plain words. The "what it's for" part matters: it lets you and the client test later requests against the goal. Example: "Redesign the five core pages of the Riverside Physio website so new patients can find prices and book an appointment on mobile."

2. Deliverables

This is the heart of the document. List every item the client will receive, with enough detail that a stranger could check whether it was delivered. Good deliverables have a number, a format and a description.

  • Weak: "Website design."
  • Strong: "Desktop and mobile designs for 5 pages (Home, Services, Prices, About, Contact), delivered as a shared design file and PDF."

If a deliverable has variable size, put a cap on it: "up to 800 words per page", "up to 12 product photos edited", "one 60-minute training session".

3. Out of scope

This is the section most freelancers skip and most regret skipping. List the things a reasonable client might assume are included but aren't. Common ones: copywriting, stock photo licences, hosting, content entry, extra pages, translations, printing, ongoing maintenance, and anything on a platform you didn't agree to work in.

You don't have to list everything in the world. List the things that came up in your conversations, and the things that came up in disputes on past projects.

4. Revisions

Say how many rounds of changes are included, what counts as a round, and what happens after that. A clear rule: "Two rounds of revisions are included per deliverable. A round is one consolidated set of feedback from the client. Further rounds are billed at $[rate] per hour." Ask for consolidated feedback in one message; it saves you both time.

5. Timeline and milestones

List key dates, but tie them to client actions. Your timeline depends on the client sending content, giving feedback and approving work. Write that down: "Designs delivered within 10 working days of receiving all page content. Client feedback due within 5 working days of each delivery. Delays in feedback move the following dates by the same amount."

6. Client responsibilities

What the client must provide, and by when: logins, brand files, written content, product information, a single decision-maker, timely feedback. Naming a single point of contact who can approve work prevents the classic problem of the CEO appearing at the last round with new opinions.

7. Acceptance

How does a deliverable count as finished? A simple approach: "A deliverable is accepted when the client approves it in writing, or 7 days after delivery if no change requests are received." Without this, projects can stay open for months.

8. Changes to scope

Requests for new work will happen. Decide now how you'll handle them: "Requests outside this scope will be quoted separately as a change request. Work on a change starts after the client approves the quote in writing." That one sentence turns an awkward conversation into a routine one. A scope of work template you can copy

Scope of Work: [Project name]
Client: [Name, company] · Freelancer: [Your name] · Date: [date] · Linked to agreement dated [date]

  1. Project summary: [What the project is, and what it's for, in 2-3 sentences.]
  2. Deliverables: [Numbered list. Each with quantity, format and description.]
  3. Out of scope: [Items not included.]
  4. Revisions: [Number] rounds per deliverable. A round is one consolidated set of feedback. Extra rounds: $[rate]/hour.
  5. Timeline: [Milestones and dates, each tied to the client action it depends on.]
  6. Client responsibilities: [What the client provides and by when. Name of the person who approves work.]
  7. Acceptance: Approved in writing, or [7] days after delivery with no change requests.
  8. Changes: Requests outside this scope are quoted separately and start after written approval.

Worked example: a five-page website redesign

Here is how the template looks filled in for a typical small project.

SectionWhat it says
SummaryRedesign 5 core pages of the Riverside Physio website so new patients can find prices and book on mobile. Built in the client's existing website builder.
Deliverables1) Sitemap and page outline for 5 pages. 2) Desktop and mobile designs for Home, Services, Prices, About, Contact. 3) The 5 pages built in the existing website builder. 4) One 45-minute video call showing staff how to edit the pages.
Out of scopeWriting page copy, new photography, blog, online booking system setup, hosting or domain changes, pages beyond the five listed, search engine advertising.
RevisionsTwo rounds on designs, one round on the built pages. Extra rounds at $75/hour.
TimelineOutline: 3 working days after kickoff. Designs: 10 working days after final copy received. Build: 7 working days after design approval. Feedback due within 5 working days each time; late feedback moves later dates.
Client responsibilitiesAll page copy and photos by 1 March. Website builder admin access at kickoff. Dr. Lee is the single approver.
AcceptanceWritten approval, or 7 days after delivery with no change requests.
ChangesNew pages or features quoted separately; work starts after written approval.

Notice what this example does. It names the platform (so "can you move us to a different builder?" is clearly new work). It caps the training. It ties every date to a client action. And the out-of-scope list includes "online booking system setup", because the client mentioned booking and could easily assume it was included.

Common scope of work mistakes

  • Describing activities instead of outputs. "Design support" or "marketing help" can't be checked. "Four social media graphics per week, in two sizes" can.
  • No caps. "Edit photos" with no number invites 300 photos. Always put a number on anything that can grow.
  • Fixed dates with no dependencies. If the client sends content three weeks late, a fixed deadline still makes you look late.
  • Unlimited revisions. They sound generous and they usually lead to resentment on both sides. Two or three rounds is common.
  • No acceptance rule. Without one, a project is never quite finished, and neither is your final invoice.
  • Writing it after the work starts. A scope agreed before work begins is a plan. A scope written halfway through is an argument.

How to present it to the client

Send the scope of work before or with your contract, and ask the client to read the deliverables and out-of-scope sections in particular. A useful line: "Could you check sections 2 and 3 especially? If anything you're expecting isn't listed, let's add it now, before we start." This invites the hard conversation while it's still cheap. Clients rarely object; most appreciate knowing exactly what they're buying.

Once agreed, keep the scope where you can both find it, and refer back to it by section number. "That sounds like a great addition. It's outside section 2, so I'll send a quick quote" is far easier to say when the document exists.

The short version

A good freelance scope of work is specific, countable and dull. It lists every deliverable with a number and format, names what's not included, limits revisions, ties dates to client actions, says how work is accepted, and explains how changes are handled. Write it before you start, keep it to a page or two, and refer to it whenever a request doesn't fit.
