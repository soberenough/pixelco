How to Raise Your Freelance Rates With Existing Clients

Raising your rate for new clients is easy: you just quote the new number. Raising it for the clients you already have feels much harder, because there's a relationship at stake and a price they've got used to. But if you never raise rates with existing clients, your best, longest-running work slowly becomes your worst-paid work. This guide walks through when to raise, by how much, how much notice to give, what to write, and how to handle the replies.

Why existing clients drift below your rate

Most freelancers set a rate when they start working with a client and then leave it there. Meanwhile, three things move:

  • Your costs. Software, insurance, equipment and everyday living costs tend to rise every year. A rate that stays flat is a pay cut in real terms.
  • Your skill. After a year with a client you work faster and know their business. They get more value per hour than they did on day one.
  • Your market. If new clients now happily pay more than your oldest ones, the gap is costing you money every month, and it limits the time you have for better-paid work.

A regular, well-communicated increase is normal business practice. Many clients expect one and budget for it. The ones that get upset are usually reacting to surprise, not to the increase itself.

Step 1: Decide whether now is the right time

Good moments to raise rates:

  • At a natural renewal point, such as the start of a new year, a new contract, a new phase or a retainer renewal.
  • After you've delivered a clear win the client noticed.
  • When you've been on the same rate for 12 months or more.
  • When you're close to fully booked and turning down work.

Moments to avoid:

  • In the middle of a fixed-price project you've already quoted. Honour the quote and apply the new rate to the next piece of work.
  • Straight after a missed deadline or a quality problem. Fix the relationship first.
  • When the client has just told you about budget cuts. Note it, and plan to revisit at the next renewal.

Before you decide anything, read your contract. If it fixes rates for a period or sets a notice requirement, follow it. If you're unsure what a clause means, get advice from someone qualified where you work.

Step 2: Work out the new number

Start from what you need, not from what you think the client will tolerate. If you haven't recalculated your rate recently, the Freelance Rate Calculator turns your income goal, costs and real billable hours into an hourly figure.

Then compare each client's current rate with that figure. A simple way to think about the size of the increase:

SituationTypical approach
Rate reviewed in the last year, small gapA modest annual adjustment, often in the region of 5-10%
No change for two years or moreA larger step, or two smaller steps six months apart
Client is far below your current rateMove to your full rate over one or two notices, or decide whether to keep them at all
Client is already at your current rateNo change this round

These ranges are a starting point, not a rule. Your field, location and demand matter more than any general figure.

A worked example: you charge one client $60 an hour for about 20 hours a month. Your current rate for new clients is $75. A 10% increase takes them to $66, which adds $120 a month, or $1,440 a year, for the same work. Moving them to $75 in two steps ($67 now, $75 in six months) closes the gap without a single large jump.

Round to a clean number. $66 or $65 reads better than $66.42.

Step 3: Choose how much notice to give

Notice is what makes a rate increase feel fair. As a general guide:

  • Hourly or ad hoc clients: at least 30 days.
  • Monthly retainers: 60 days, or one full billing cycle plus a month.
  • Clients with annual budgets: give notice before their budgeting period if you can. Ask early in the conversation when they plan next year's spend.

Pick a specific effective date, such as "from 1 February". Avoid vague phrasing like "early next year".

Step 4: Write the notice

Keep it short, confident and specific. A good rate-increase message has five parts:

  1. A line of thanks or a reference to the work you've done together.
  2. The new rate, stated plainly.
  3. The date it takes effect.
  4. What stays the same, such as current projects at the quoted price.
  5. An offer to talk, without asking permission.

Here is an example you can adapt:

Hi Sam,

It's been a great year working on the newsletter with you, and I'm glad the new format has been landing well with your readers.

I'm writing to let you know that from 1 February my rate for ongoing work will be $66 an hour, up from $60. This is my first change since we started working together, and it reflects the costs of running my business and the time I now put into each issue.

Anything we've already agreed before then will stay at the current rate. If you'd like to talk about scope or budget for next year, I'm happy to set up a quick call.

Thanks again,
Alex

What to leave out:

  • Long justifications. One sentence of reasoning is enough. Three paragraphs reads as if you expect a fight.
  • Apologies. "Sorry to do this" invites negotiation.
  • Questions that ask for permission. "Would that be okay?" turns a notice into a request.

Send it by email so there's a written record, even if you also mention it on a call. If the client prefers to talk, a short call followed by the same email in writing works well.

Step 5: Handle the replies

Most clients reply with a simple acknowledgement. When they don't, the reply usually falls into one of these patterns.

"Can we keep the old rate a bit longer?"

You can say yes to a short delay without dropping the increase. For example: "I can hold the current rate until 1 March, then the new rate applies." This keeps the increase and gives them breathing room.

"Our budget is fixed."

Offer to change scope rather than price: "I understand. At the new rate, your current budget covers about 18 hours a month instead of 20. Shall we look at which tasks to prioritise?" This keeps your rate intact and helps them plan.

"Other freelancers charge less."

Don't argue about other people's prices. Point to what's specific about working with you: you know their business, their tools and their audience, and switching has its own cost in time and risk. Then let the decision be theirs.

"We can't do it."

Decide your answer before you send the notice. Either you hold the old rate for this client for a set time, or you're prepared to part ways. If you part ways, offer a clean handover and a reasonable end date. A professional exit often brings the client back later, or brings a referral.

What if you've undercharged for years?

If a client is far below your current rate, one large jump can be a shock. Two approaches work well:

  • Stepped increases. Announce both steps in one message: "From 1 February my rate will be $67, and from 1 August it will be $75." The client can plan for both, and you only have the conversation once.
  • Restructure the work. Move from hourly to a monthly retainer or a per-project price at the new level. A change of structure can make a change of price easier to accept.

Also ask yourself whether this client still fits. If they would only stay at a rate well below your others, freeing that time for better-paid work may be the stronger choice.

Make it a routine

The easiest rate increase is the one clients expect. A few habits make that happen:

  • Review every client's rate once a year, on the same date.
  • Mention in new contracts or proposals that rates are reviewed annually.
  • Keep a simple list of each client, their current rate, the date it last changed and the next review date.
  • Check how much each client actually earns you per hour, not just the headline rate. The Project Profit Checker shows where unbilled time is eating into a project.

A quick checklist

  1. Check your contract for fixed-rate periods or notice terms.
  2. Recalculate your current rate from your costs and real billable hours.
  3. Decide each client's new rate and round it to a clean number.
  4. Pick an effective date with at least 30 days' notice (60 for retainers).
  5. Write a short notice: thanks, new rate, date, what stays the same, offer to talk.
  6. Decide in advance what you'll do if a client says no.
  7. Send it in writing and update your invoices from the effective date.
  8. Put next year's review date in your calendar.

Raising rates with existing clients is a small, uncomfortable conversation once a year. Skipping it is a cost you pay every month. Give clear notice, keep the message short, and treat it as the routine part of running a business that it is.

This guide is general information, not legal or financial advice. Check your own contracts and local rules before changing your terms.
