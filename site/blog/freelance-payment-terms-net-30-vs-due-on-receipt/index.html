Freelance Payment Terms Explained: Net 30, Net 15 or Due on Receipt?

"Payment terms" is just the agreement about when a client pays your invoice. It's one line on your invoice and one clause in your contract, yet it decides how long you wait for money you've already earned. Choose Net 30 by habit and you may be financing your clients for a month on every job. Choose terms that are too tight for a large organisation and your invoice may simply sit in a queue anyway.

This guide explains what the common terms mean, how to pick the right one for each client, how to write them clearly, and what to do when a client insists on longer terms. It's general information, not legal or financial advice. Rules on late payment and interest vary by country, so check your local requirements before adding penalties to an invoice.

The short answer

  • Small clients and individuals: Due on receipt or Net 7.
  • Small and medium businesses: Net 14 or Net 15 is a reasonable default for most freelancers.
  • Large companies with formal accounts teams: Expect Net 30 or longer. Protect yourself with a deposit and milestone invoices rather than fighting their cycle.
  • Whatever you choose: Agree it before work starts, put it in the contract, and write an actual due date on every invoice.

"Net" simply means the full amount. "Net 30" is the full amount within 30 days. The days counted are calendar days, not working days, unless your contract says otherwise.

Why "Due on receipt" isn't always fastest

It sounds like the quickest option, and with individuals and small business owners who pay their own bills, it often is. But inside larger companies, "Due on receipt" can backfire. Invoices go through an approval process and then into a scheduled payment run. An invoice with no specific date can drift to the bottom of the pile, because nobody can see when it's actually late.

A clear date such as "Due June 15" is often paid more reliably than "Due on receipt," because it gives the accounts team a deadline to work to and gives you a clear point at which to follow up.

How to choose terms for each client

Ask four questions:

  1. Who pays the invoice? If the person who hired you also pays the bills, short terms work. If an accounts payable team does, ask what their standard cycle is before you quote.
  2. How big is the invoice compared with your monthly costs? The bigger the invoice, the more a long wait hurts. Large projects need a deposit and milestone invoices regardless of the terms.
  3. How well do you know the client? New clients with no payment history get shorter terms or more upfront. Long-standing clients who always pay on time can have more flexibility.
  4. What can your cash flow handle? If every client paid on the last possible day, could you still cover rent and bills? If not, your terms are too long.

A simple way to think about it: your payment terms plus the client's typical lateness equals how long you're really waiting. Net 30 with a client who usually pays a week late means waiting more than five weeks after you finish work. How to write payment terms clearly

On the invoice

Always show both the term and the actual date. Don't make the client do arithmetic.

"Invoice date: June 1. Payment terms: Net 14. Due date: June 15."

Put the due date near the total, where the eye goes first. Include your payment details on the invoice itself so nobody has to search old emails for them.

In the contract

A good payment clause answers five questions: how much, when it's invoiced, when it's due, how to pay, and what happens if it's late. For example:

"Fees are invoiced as follows: 50% on signing and 50% on final delivery. Invoices are payable within 14 days of the invoice date by bank transfer to the account shown on the invoice. Overdue invoices may incur a late fee as set out below, and work may be paused until any overdue balance is paid."

If you add a late fee or interest, check what's allowed where you and your client are based. Some countries set statutory interest or compensation for late payments between businesses, and some places limit what you can charge. Whatever you do, make sure the client agrees to it in the contract before the work starts; a fee that appears for the first time on an overdue invoice is hard to enforce and harms the relationship. In the proposal

One line is enough: "Payment terms: 50% deposit on signing, balance due 14 days after delivery." Clients should never see your terms for the first time on an invoice.

What to do when the client wants longer terms

Large clients may tell you their payment terms are Net 45, Net 60 or more, and that they can't change them. Sometimes that's true. You often can't change their cycle, but you can change how much money is waiting in it.

  • Ask first. "Is that fixed, or is there a faster route for small suppliers?" Some organisations have shorter terms for small businesses or sole traders.
  • Invoice earlier. Send the deposit invoice on the day the contract is signed, not on day one of the work. The clock starts sooner.
  • Break the work into milestones. Three invoices on a 60-day cycle are much easier on your cash flow than one invoice at the end.
  • Price it in. Waiting two months to be paid has a real cost to you. It's reasonable to reflect that in your quote.
  • Get set up as a supplier early. Many companies need tax forms, bank details and a purchase order number before they can pay at all. Ask what they need during onboarding, not when the first invoice is due.

Should you offer an early payment discount?

Terms like "2/10 Net 30" reward clients for paying quickly. They can work with businesses that actively look for discounts, but think it through before offering one. A 2% discount for paying 20 days early is an expensive way to borrow money, and some clients take the discount and pay late anyway. For most freelancers, shorter terms plus a deposit do the same job without giving money away. If you do offer a discount, state the exact discounted amount and the exact date it expires.

Common mistakes

  • Defaulting to Net 30 because it sounds professional. Short terms are just as professional. Choose them on purpose.
  • Different terms in the contract and the invoice. If they conflict, expect the client to pick the longer one.
  • No due date on the invoice. Write the date, not just "Net 14".
  • Not following up on the due date. Send a friendly reminder the day after it's due. Waiting weeks teaches clients that your due dates are flexible. Our guide on following up on an unpaid invoice covers what to say at each stage.
  • Waiting until the end to invoice. On long projects, invoice as you go.

A quick setup checklist

  1. Pick your default terms (Net 14 is a good starting point) and your exceptions for individuals and large companies.
  2. Add a payment clause to your contract template covering amount, timing, method and lateness.
  3. Add a one-line payment section to your proposal template.
  4. Update your invoice template so it shows the term and the due date next to the total.
  5. Set a reminder to follow up the day after each due date.
