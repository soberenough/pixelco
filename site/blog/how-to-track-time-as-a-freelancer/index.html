How to Track Your Time as a Freelancer (Even If You Charge by the Project)

Most freelancers know they should track their time. Fewer do it consistently, and many who bill by the project don't do it at all, because nobody is asking for a timesheet. But your hours are the raw material of your business. Without them you can't tell whether a price was right, which client is quietly costing you money, or why a busy month didn't pay as well as you expected. This guide covers what to track, how to make it a habit that takes a few minutes a day, and how to turn the numbers into better invoices and better quotes.

Why track time if you don't bill hourly?

If you bill by the hour, the reason is obvious: your log is your invoice. If you charge fixed prices, tracking still answers three questions nothing else can:

  • Was that price right? A $2,000 project that took 20 hours paid you $100 an hour. The same fee for 45 hours paid about $44. You only know which one you had if you counted.
  • Where does scope creep happen? When a project runs long, your log shows whether the extra hours came from new requests or from work that was harder than expected. One needs a conversation with the client; the other needs a higher quote next time.
  • How much of your week is unpaid? Proposals, invoicing, email and admin are real work. If they take a third of your week, your rate has to cover them.

What to record for each block of work

Keep it simple. Each time you start a different task, you need one row with:

Write task names a client could read. "Homepage revisions, round 1" can go straight onto an invoice; "fixing stuff" can't. And spell client names the same way every time, or your totals won't add up.

Pick a tracking method you'll actually keep

The best method is the one you'll still be using in a month. Three approaches work for most people:

1. Live logging

Keep a spreadsheet or timer open. When you switch tasks, note the end time and start a new row. It's the most accurate method and suits hourly billing, especially with clients who ask for detail.

2. Jot it down, transfer it later

Keep a notepad or a plain text note beside you. Each time you switch, write the time and a few words: "10:30 bakery print files". At the end of the day, take three minutes to move it into your log. This is the easiest way to start, because it barely interrupts your work.

3. Calendar blocks

If you already plan your day in a calendar, adjust each block at the end of the day to match what really happened, then copy the blocks into your log. It works well for people whose days are mostly scheduled.

Whichever you choose, give it two full weeks before you judge it. Most people quit in the first few days because they're trying to account for every minute. You don't need to. Accurate to the nearest quarter hour is plenty.

Set your rounding rules once

Decide how you round and stick to it, so your invoices are consistent and easy to explain:

  • Quarter hour (0.25) is the most common choice.
  • Six minutes (0.1) is common among consultants billing larger organisations.
  • Round each block on its own, to the nearest increment, not always up. A client who sees a long run of 0.25-hour emails will start asking questions.
  • Bundle tiny tasks. Ten five-minute client emails is nearly an hour. Log them as one row at the end of the day, such as "Client email (misc)".

Decide what counts as billable before the work starts

Disagreements about invoices usually come from unspoken assumptions. Put your rules in your proposal or contract. A typical split looks like this:

  • Usually billable: project work, client meetings and calls, revisions, research for that client, project email.
  • Billable if agreed up front: travel time, quick ad-hoc calls, small "can you just" requests.
  • Usually not billable: proposals, invoicing, your own admin, and fixing your own mistakes.

Even when time isn't billable, log it. It's still part of what your rate has to pay for.

The three numbers worth watching

Once you have a couple of weeks of data, a short Friday review turns your log into decisions. Three numbers do most of the work.

Utilisation

Client hours (billable plus fixed-price) divided by total hours worked. If you worked 25 hours and 21 were on client work, that's 84%. Very low utilisation means too much unpaid time; consistently very high utilisation can mean you're not leaving room for sales and marketing, which shows up later as a quiet month.

Effective hourly rate

Everything you earned in the period divided by every hour you worked, including admin. This is the honest number. Say you bill $60 an hour, earned $615 from hourly work and the equivalent of $700 from a fixed-price project, and worked 25.25 hours in total. Your effective rate is $1,315 divided by 25.25, about $52. Below target, and now you know to look at why.

Budget used versus work done

For each fixed-price project, divide the fee by your target hourly rate to get a budget in hours. A $2,800 project at $60 an hour gives you roughly 47 hours. Then compare the share of hours used to the share of work finished. If you've used half the hours but done less than half the work, the project is drifting, and it's far easier to raise that with the client at the halfway point than at the end. A 15-minute Friday review

  1. Tidy the week. Fill any gaps from your calendar, sent email and file history while you still remember.
  2. Total it up. Billable, fixed-price and non-billable hours; revenue; utilisation; effective rate.
  3. Check fixed-price projects. Any where hours used is running ahead of work done?
  4. Look at admin. If it's eating more than roughly 15% of your week, what can you batch, template or drop?
  5. Choose one change for next week. Only one. Write it down.

Often the one change isn't "work faster". It's "send a change request for the extra page the client asked for on Wednesday." That single conversation can be worth more than a week of efficiency gains.

Turning your log into a clear invoice

Clients pay faster when an invoice matches what they expected and doesn't invite questions. Filter your log to one client, one billing period and billable rows only. Then group the rows into a few lines by project or type of work, rather than pasting in forty entries. For example:

  • Spring menu design: layout options, review call, final artwork and print files, 6.25 hours
  • Instagram template set, including one round of revisions (approved 5 Nov), 4 hours

Label any extra work with the date it was approved, check that the hours add up to your log, and keep the detailed extract ready in case the client asks to see it. Common mistakes

  • Reconstructing the week on Friday. Memory is good for about a day. Log daily, even if it's rough.
  • Too many categories. Keep the list under ten, or the totals stop meaning anything.
  • Only tracking billable work. Then your effective rate looks better than it is.
  • Collecting data you never read. The log is only useful if you spend fifteen minutes a week looking at it.

The short version

Log every block of work with a client, a task name a client could read, a billable status and the hours. Round consistently. Once a week, check your utilisation, your effective rate and your fixed-price budgets, and pick one thing to change. Do that for a month and you'll quote with more confidence, catch scope creep early, and know exactly what your time is worth.
